OpenAI’s Navier–Stokes paper in plain terms

A whirlpool that spins up to infinite speed

In September 2026 OpenAI posted a 166-page proof about the equations of fluid flow. It builds a swirling flow that starts at rest, gets a smooth push in a limited region for a limited time, and reaches infinite speed at a set moment while its total energy stays finite. Mathematicians call this a blow-up. Whether one can happen is the question behind the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem.

Finite time blowup for Navier–Stokes·166 pages·announced 8 Sep 2026

The equation and the claim

Navier–Stokes is Newton’s second law for a fluid that cannot be squeezed. Here u is the fluid’s velocity, p its pressure, ν its viscosity and f an outside push.

∂tuhow the flow changes + (u·∇)uthe fluid carries itself along − νΔufriction smooths it out + ∇ppressure = foutside push
∇·u = 0cannot be squeezed u(x, 0) = 0starts at rest

The term (u·∇)u is the only nonlinear one. Almost all of the difficulty sits there, and it is also the term the paper’s ripples exploit.

Theorem 1.1 in words

For every viscosity ν > 0 there is a smooth push f, acting in a bounded region for a bounded time, such that a fluid starting at rest reaches infinite speed at time t = 1 while its kinetic energy stays bounded. So with that push, no smooth solution with bounded energy can exist for all time.

How the paper builds it

The proof works backwards. It chooses the flow first, then shows that the push this flow needs is smooth.

  1. Step 1

    Design the whirlpool first

    The paper writes down a spinning column in which fluid spirals inward toward the axis and shoots out along it, upward above a thin middle layer and downward below it. The shape stays the same as it shrinks, so the rate of collapse can be written down exactly.

    In the paper: §2.1, §§4–5

  2. Step 2

    Read off the push it needs

    Any flow you choose satisfies Navier–Stokes if you define the push f as whatever is left over in the equation. The catch is that the push must stay smooth. For the bare whirlpool, the leftover grows without limit in a ring around the core as t approaches 1, so it is not allowed.

    In the paper: §2, §2.2, eq. (3.1)

  3. Step 3

    Add ripples in the ring

    Small ring-shaped waves are placed where the leftover is too large. The whirlpool’s shear makes them grow, then viscosity damps them. Each ripple averages to zero, yet the momentum it carries does not, and that transport supplies exactly the push the ring was missing. In effect the fluid pushes itself. Two families of ripples cover the two directions of the missing push, and new ones are added at ever finer scales as blow-up nears.

    In the paper: §2.2, §§6–7, Proposition 7.5

  4. Step 4

    Repeat until only a smooth push is left

    Each round of corrections leaves a smaller error for the next round to cancel. Summed up, the leftover and all its derivatives fade to zero at the blow-up point. The flow is then cut off smoothly in space, and the calm swirl outside needs no push at all. What remains is a smooth push that can be continued past t = 1 and then switched off.

    In the paper: §§8–10, Theorem 1.1

Why the energy stays finite

The core speeds up, but its volume shrinks faster than its speed squared grows.

CoreScales likeAs τ → 0
Widthτ1/2→ 0
Heightτ1/2−h→ 0
Top speedτ−1/2−h→ ∞
Volumeτ3/2−h→ 0
Energyτ1/2−3h→ 0

Energy ≈ volume × speed² = τ3/2−h · τ−1−2h = τ1/2−3h, which goes to 0.

τ = 1 − t, 0 < h < 1/100, ℓr/ℓz ≍ τh → 0 (a thin column). Whole flow: ‖u(t)‖22 + 2∫t0‖∇u‖22 ds ≤ ℱ(t)2, ℱ(t) = ∫t0‖f(s)‖2 ds < ∞ (Lemma 10.4).

What it would and would not show

If the proof holds

  • A smooth, local, short-lived push can drive a 3D viscous fluid from rest to infinite speed in finite time, with finite energy.
  • This works for every viscosity, both in all of space and in a periodic box.
  • Clay’s official problem statement lists four acceptable answers: two smoothness theorems (A, B) and two breakdown examples (C, D). The paper claims (C), and (D) for the box.

What it does not show

  • Whether a fluid with no outside push can blow up by itself. The paper does not address that case.
  • Anything about natural forces. The push is custom-built for this one flow.
